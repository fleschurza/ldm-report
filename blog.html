UDHR Art. 23 — Right to Work & Dignity
"streamlining" — plain language, right next to it

Enphase Energy's Form 8-K, filed November 20, frames a cut of about 12% of its global workforce — the filing states this is approximately 320 employees — as "streamlining its manufacturing operations" and consolidating its facilities footprint. The CEO's letter to staff, filed the same day, uses a different register: "We have informed approximately 320 impacted employees," it states, before setting out pay through a named separation date (generally December 11), a minimum of 13 weeks' severance, and healthcare continuing through the end of that month.

"Streamlining" is the abstraction here — a frictionless metaphor that doesn't name the people who lose their roles in the process it describes. What's notable is that the same filing doesn't stop at that word: it names the headcount, the date, and the exact severance terms, in plain language, rather than leaving employees to learn them later.

UDHR Art. 23 — Right to Work & Dignity
"downsizing"

Seatrium, the Singapore marine and offshore engineering group, announced today a "workforce redesign programme" that will cut about 2% of its Singapore workforce — the company confirmed this is roughly 220 people. Chief Executive Chris Ong's statement on the move: "We are not downsizing or simply cost cutting. We are repositioning and readying Seatrium to capture new opportunities."

"Downsizing" is already a euphemism — a size metaphor standing in for people losing their income. What's notable here is that the statement names that euphemism only to reach past it, toward "repositioning," to describe the same 220 jobs. The rest of the statement does similar work: "streamlining," "optimisation," and "restructuring" each describe the process, not the person leaving it.

"cost savings"

MySize, Inc. (Nasdaq: MYSZ) reported its third-quarter 2026 results on November 20, disclosing that it cut the workforce at its Naiz Fit subsidiary by approximately 50% this September — the company estimates this will save about $793,000 a year. The release frames the cut under the header "Operational Efficiency and Cost Optimization."

The 50% figure is stated plainly in the release; what's named only abstractly is everything around it. "restructuring"

Upwork's Form 8-K/A, filed November 12, expands a "Restructuring Plan" first announced in May: the board approved extending it on November 10, and combined with a separate cut at a subsidiary, the total workforce reduction is now about 37%, up from the 24% first disclosed. The filing moves completion to the first quarter of 2027, from the fourth quarter of 2026.

The document uses the word "restructuring" ten times in about 730 words — on a document this size, a density of roughly 14 per 1,000 words — without once naming what happens to the people affected. "Restructuring" names a corporate reorganisation; it doesn't name the physical consequence for the people reorganised out of it. The filing is, in that sense, doing what it's required to do: disclosing a material event in the vocabulary SEC filings use. The gap between that vocabulary and what "37% of the workforce" means for each of those people is the subject of this entry.

"attrition" — plain language, right next to it

Boeing's November 9 memo on cutting about 5% of its commercial-airplane workforce describes the reduction as coming "through a combination of attrition and involuntary layoffs."

"Attrition" is the abstraction here — it names natural turnover, not firing, and gets used precisely because it avoids naming the people being let go. What's worth noting: the same sentence doesn't rely on that word alone. It names the other half of the reduction directly — "involuntary layoffs" — the actual action, not a softer word for it. This is what the plain half of a sentence looks like, sitting right next to the abstracted half.

Planetary Boundaries PB-1 — Climate Change
"energy transition"

A market-research release this month — MarketsandMarkets' forecast for the smart greenhouse market, projected to reach $4.13 billion by 2032 — cites "government-led energy transition policies" among its demand drivers, alongside gains in "operational efficiency."

"Energy transition" suggests an orderly shift between energy systems — it doesn't name the physical harm to communities that can occur during the transition itself: a town losing its one employer, a region's grid strained during the changeover. "Operational efficiency" does similar work for cost: the physical costs transferred to workers and ecosystems, named only as a percentage saved.

2026-11-16 Euphemism flagged
Planetary Boundaries PB-1 — Climate Change
"net zero"

A climate-finance benchmark released today — Senken's Pace of Change 2026, covering Europe's largest companies — puts a number on a gap the phrase itself doesn't name: actually removing the emissions that these companies' net-zero targets imply would cost roughly €51–68 billion a year, and few firms are buying removals anywhere near that scale yet.

"Net zero" permits continued emissions as long as an equivalent amount is theoretically offset elsewhere — it doesn't by itself require that the offset be permanent, independently verified, or purchased in the volume the target implies. The benchmark measures exactly that distance between the accounting term and what it would take to make the atmosphere whole.
