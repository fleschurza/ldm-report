Method

What unmask.tools maintains, how an entry qualifies, and where the current approach is known to be weak.

Draft, not yet externally reviewed. We're publishing this as-is rather than holding it back pending review, so the method is visible from the start and corrections can come from anyone reading it. Questions or corrections: hello@unmask.tools.

1. What this is and what it is not

unmask.tools maintains a reference library of phrases that abstract away a concrete human consequence, and a browser extension (unmask.shield) that marks those phrases while people read or write.

The recurring question is one: what does this wording make a reader stop seeing? "Collateral damage" is a phrase that removes dead civilians from the sentence. "Rückführung" can remove a person being forced to leave a country.

"Physical" in this project is deliberately broad. It covers not only bodily injury but the physical conditions of a life — a body, a home, a livelihood, a place to live — that a phrase can abstract away. A phrase about job losses or administrative reclassification leaves out a different kind of physical consequence than a phrase about killing, but both leave out something that happens to a body or to the material conditions it depends on. Severity (section 4) is what distinguishes the two; the word "physical" by itself does not rank them. It is not a claim that all abstraction is bad.

The library does not:

  • decide whether a statement is true or false
  • claim that anyone who uses a phrase intends to hide something
  • list words that must not be used
  • judge people, organisations or outlets

A phrase can be used legitimately — for example when quoting a treaty, reporting, or criticising the phrase. The tool marks the phrase, and the reader decides.

2. Definitions

TermMeaning in this project
PhraseA word or multi-word expression in a given language
AbstractionThe phrase names a category, process or method instead of the concrete consequence
Concrete referentWhat actually happens to people or their living conditions (for example "civilians are killed")
Plain translationOne-line statement of the concrete referent, in everyday words
Legal anchorA primary legal or normative text that names the concrete referent (Geneva Conventions, Rome Statute, Genocide Convention, ICERD, UDHR, Planetary Boundaries)
SeverityWhich kind of consequence the phrase abstracts (see section 4)

3. Inclusion criteria for an entry

An entry is included when all of the following hold:

  1. Use: the phrase occurs in public institutional, corporate or political language, with at least one dated, primary-source example.
  2. Referent: a concrete referent can be named in plain words.
  3. Anchor: a legal or normative text can be cited that names that referent.
  4. Confirmation: for phrases that enter via the community path, promotion thresholds apply (confirmations by independent users and across domains: critical 2 confirmations / 1 domain, high 3 / 2, medium 3 / 3).

Symmetry rule. The same criteria apply regardless of the political direction of the phrase or who uses it. A phrase is kept or removed because of its function, never because of its speaker.

4. Severity

Current definitions, also published in the FAQ:

  • Critical — language that abstracts killing, torture, forced detention, mass deportation, ecocide, or planetary tipping-point transgression. Anchored to the Rome Statute, the Genocide Convention, and the absolute prohibitions of the Geneva Conventions.
  • High — language that abstracts serious but non-lethal harm (forced displacement, ecosystem collapse, mass loss of livelihood, deprivation of essential resources). Anchored to UDHR economic and social rights, ICERD, and Planetary Boundaries.
  • Medium — language that abstracts institutional and structural harm (greenwashing, labour abstraction, governance euphemism, colonial-pattern framing); harmful in aggregate and systemically rather than in a single instance.

Known weakness. These are judgments, and the extension's internal risk weighting is a convenience for comparing documents chosen by the author, not a measurement derived from evidence. We're asking reviewers whether to keep it, replace it, or drop it.

5. Entry format (target)

Every public dictionary entry is meant to carry: phrase and language, a neutral explanation of what the phrase does (no verdict), a plain translation, the concrete referent, a legal anchor with the article cited, a severity and the rule that placed it there, dated primary-source examples, a note on legitimate or contested uses, a review status (draft / reviewed by name and date / disputed), and a version number with change log. Not every entry has all of this yet — it's the target the dictionary is being brought up to, not a claim about today's state.

6. How detection works, and where it fails

Detection runs in two stages: static matching against the library (fast, local, no data leaves the device), and an optional AI path for phrases the list misses, used only when the reader opts in.

Known error classes, not yet systematically measured:

  • quotation, negation and criticism of a phrase are flagged like use
  • inflection and compounds, especially in German
  • context-dependent phrases that are neutral in some domains
  • the AI path can invent or over-state a match

Readers can mark a detection "Real use" or "Figure of speech" in the extension. These judgments are collected and will, above a minimum volume, surface a "disputed" or "mostly figurative" label on an entry — but no judgment, automated or human-adjacent, ever removes or demotes a phrase on its own. Any change to an entry is a human decision, recorded in that entry's change log.

7. Evaluation plan (not yet done)

  1. Draw a sample of 200 passages from public documents across sources and political directions.
  2. Two annotators label them independently, using this method note.
  3. Report agreement (Cohen's kappa) and where the annotators disagree.
  4. Run the detector on the same sample and report precision and recall, per language.
  5. Publish results including failures, and re-run on each major version.

8. Governance

  • Independence. No customer, funder or partner can add, remove or reword an entry. Paying customers use the same corrections process as everyone else.
  • Funding. The project is financed by subscriptions, licences and training services.
  • Corrections. Anyone can contest an entry. Disputed entries show a visible "disputed" status until reviewed. Reviewer disagreements are published, not removed.
  • Versioning. Each entry and this method have version numbers and a change log. Dataset snapshots are monthly.
  • Licensing. Dictionary text is CC BY-SA 4.0 (attribution, share-alike). Machine-readable bulk data and commercial use fall under the existing data licence tiers.

9. Open questions

These are the questions we most want a careful reader to push back on:

  1. Is the definition (abstraction away from a concrete consequence) workable and distinct enough to be tested?
  2. Are the legal anchors correct and appropriate, especially for non-lethal and ecological phrases?
  3. Does the symmetry rule hold when you look at the current entries? Which ones fail it?
  4. Is severity defensible, and should a numeric risk score exist at all?
  5. Where is the line between marking a phrase and implying a judgment about the person who used it?
  6. For entries in your language: which are wrong, missing, or too contested to include?

Send corrections or answers to hello@unmask.tools — every reply is read, and disagreements get published, not quietly dropped.
